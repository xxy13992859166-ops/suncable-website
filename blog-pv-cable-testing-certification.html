PV Cable Testing & Certification: Complete Guide for 2026

📅 October 28, 2026 ⏱ 4 min read 🏭 By JunlinChicheng Team

Three different things get called "certification" in the PV cable trade: a standard, a type test, and a certificate. They are not interchangeable, and a supplier who cannot separate them usually cannot produce the paperwork either. Here is what each one actually is, and what it does and does not prove about the drum you are buying.

The standard defines the test, not the product

IEC 62930 and its European counterpart EN 50618 describe a halogen-free, cross-linked photovoltaic cable and the tests it must pass. The standard is a document; it does not certify anything. A manufacturer can design to it, and an independent laboratory can test against it — those are separate steps.

What the type tests actually put the cable through

  • Thermal endurance. Long-duration ageing at elevated temperature, assessed to IEC 60216, is what supports a 25-year service claim. Without it the claim is marketing.
  • Cold bend and impact. The cable is conditioned at −40 °C and bent around a mandrel; a compound that cracks in a northern winter fails here.
  • UV and weather resistance. Accelerated exposure in a weathering chamber, followed by mechanical checks. This is the test that separates XLPO from PVC.
  • Ozone, water immersion and shrinkage. Small-sample tests that catch compound and cross-linking problems.
  • Voltage withstand. Proof that the insulation wall holds at the rated voltage with margin.

TÜV, UL and CE are three different claims

MarkIssued byWhat it provesHow to check
IEC 62930 / EN 50618Nobody — it is a standardThat a test regime exists for the product typeRead the scope; ask for the test report
TÜV certificateTÜV Rheinland, TÜV SÜD or similarType-tested product, with a certificate number and size scopeCertipedia or the body's own database

CE markingThe manufacturer, self-declaredDeclaration of conformity to applicable EU directivesThe declaration document itself

CE is the one most often misread. It is a self-declaration, not a third-party approval; on its own it says nothing about thermal endurance or UV performance. TÜV and UL are third-party, which is why buyers in Europe and North America ask for them by name.

What to ask a supplier, in order

  1. The certificate number, the issuing body, and the size range covered.
  2. The type-test report for thermal endurance and UV, with the test laboratory named.
  3. Confirmation that the production address on the certificate is the address that will make your order.
  4. A batch test certificate for the actual production run, not the type test from years ago.

The scope trap. A certificate that lists 4 mm² does not certify 6 mm², and a certificate held by a trading company does not transfer to the factory that makes the cable. Read the scope line, not the logo on the website.
