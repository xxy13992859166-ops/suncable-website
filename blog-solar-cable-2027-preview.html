Three changes worth planning for now

Nobody can forecast a cable price a year out, and this page will not pretend to. What can be said with confidence is that three technical directions are already visible in tenders, and each changes what a buyer should specify. They are worth planning for because they change drawings, not because they change headlines.

1. Higher DC system voltages

1500 V DC systems are now ordinary on utility-scale work, and the cable and connector on the DC side must both be rated for it. The mistake to avoid is a 1500 V connector on a 1000 V cable, or the reverse — the system is only as rated as its weakest link. When you write a specification, state the system voltage and require the certificate to cover it.

2. Storage coupled on the DC side

More projects now put batteries behind the same DC bus as the array. That adds a cable duty the PV side never had: high continuous current at low voltage, in an enclosure, often indoors. It is a different product from PV cable — low-smoke halogen-free construction, sized for current rather than voltage drop — and it should be a separate line on the bill of materials.

DirectionWhat it changes in your specification
1500 V DCCable and connector rated and certified at 1500 V, not just the inverter
DC-coupled storageSeparate LSZH battery cable, sized on current
TraceabilityBatch test certificate per drum, production address on the certificate

3. Traceability from certificate to drum

Inspection regimes are asking a question that used to be answered with a logo: which factory made this drum, and does the certificate name it? The practical preparation is unglamorous — keep the certificate scope by size, the batch test report, and the production address together, and make sure they refer to the same entity. Suppliers who cannot produce that set will increasingly be filtered out at tender stage, not at delivery.

What does not change

The fundamentals stay: XLPO insulation, tinned copper Class 5 conductor, a certificate that covers the size you are buying, and a joint made with the right tool. Any specification that sacrifices those for a new feature is trading a certain benefit for an uncertain one.
